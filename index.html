the operating thesis

Engineering as a P&L, not a cost center

Every system my team ships carries a live financial model behind it. Value is decomposed into nine channels — revenue protected, client retention, media efficiency, error prevention, billing accuracy, risk avoided, labour returned, cost avoided, and new revenue — and every dollar is a formula pointed at auditable inputs, each rated for confidence. No asserted totals, anywhere. The most recent audit of that model revised my own headline number downward, on purpose: a figure you can defend in front of a CFO beats a bigger one you can't.

That discipline changes what gets built. Builds are weighed the way finance weighs them — what they do to COGS, what margin they protect, what they contribute to EBITDA — and some have matured into intellectual property with standalone value: a metered service that turned an internal tool into a billable product line, and a growing proprietary dataset that compounds in value as it accrues. A few of the quieter examples: a daily compliance sweep across roughly 1,400 ad accounts that catches committed media running dark, a four-hour reconciliation loop across eight platforms that flags money moving with no sold line behind it, and a privacy toolkit that lets client data meet third-party AI with cryptographic guarantees instead of good intentions.

None of it is a solo act. I lead a tight-knit team of driving engineers who own their systems end to end — and the operating rule is that nothing counts until it's running in production for real users, valued honestly, and adopted by the people it serves.

about

The shape of the work

I lead engineering, AI, and innovation at Arena, a digital advertising firm with deep political-cycle exposure and an unusually broad surface area (DSPs, social, CTV, direct mail). The role was created on purpose: a distinct vector for engineering excellence next to traditional agency work.

I'm most useful at the intersection of three things — production AI systems that have to actually run, hard data and information-theoretic problems, and the human side of getting an entire organization to adopt new machinery. I own the AI strategy end to end: I authored our enterprise AI Policy v2.0 (7 core principles, 16-category Universal Data Restrictions, vendor-assessment rubric, 24-hour incident protocol), chair the AI and Automation committees, deploy the agents themselves — secured, governed, and monitored — and run the training that gets 100+ people actually using them. Strategy on paper is cheap; I'm accountable for the execution.

Outside work I build embedded electronics, run statistical tests on PRNGs because it's fun, contribute to local political campaigns, and keep a homelab in northern Utah running on more YAML than is strictly defensible.
